## 1. Book-level decision

**Keep the three-part progression: economic choices and tools → demand → supply and market equilibrium. Rebuild the teaching and independent-practice route within it.**

The first edition already has a useful conceptual order. Its table of contents has twelve paragraphs across these three chapters, and its foreword deliberately places formal costs, revenue and marginal analysis later. There is no need to move those topics back into Book 1 just to resemble the later books. [S1, pp. 3–5]

The main second-edition change is a more explicit mathematical progression, paired with clearer limits on what counts as independent learning. Percentages, units, table differences, substitution, graph construction, intercepts and equation solving should each have a visible teaching location and later independent evidence. They should not be presumed secure because students have taken mathematics, or left to optional online help.

Do not introduce a long, abstract algebra chapter at the beginning. Teach a small amount of formula use in Chapter 1.1, apply it to a meaningful demand relation in Chapter 1.2, and then teach the two-sided equilibrium equation in Chapter 1.3. This distributes the difficulty without hiding it.

The common style with Books 2–4 comes from the **explanation–practice relationship**, not from identical chapter lengths or adding advanced topics. The new edition adopts their problem-first writing, staged visuals, optional printed support, independent practice, capstone exercises and separate answers.

### Why not change the number of chapters or paragraphs?

Retaining twelve paragraphs preserves a compact first-book scope and keeps the existing numerical references usable in Books 2–4. It does not mean preserving the wording or every current target. The capacity for better foundations comes partly from reducing unnecessary formal work in individual and collective demand, and partly from planning realistic lesson time. A paragraph is not automatically one lesson.

A fourth mathematics chapter would delay the economic applications. A fourth costs/profit chapter would duplicate formal Book 2 teaching. Conversely, merging demand and equilibrium into fewer, larger paragraphs would give beginners more simultaneous operations. None of those changes is justified by the dependency problems identified here.

## 3. Intended exit profile

By the end of Book 1, students should be able to independently:

1. Explain a constrained choice using scarcity and the best feasible alternative forgone, without treating every unchosen option as an additional cost.
2. Read a source's variables, units, period, comparison base and relevant figures; distinguish information from a conclusion that requires reasoning.
3. Calculate an amount per unit, an absolute change, a percentage change and an index; distinguish percentage points, index points and relative percentage changes.
4. Substitute values into a supplied formula, solve a simple linear relation for the missing value, and check the result against the source and its units.
5. Read and construct the required table/graph representations, including a usable scale, linear interpolation, axis intercepts and basic rectangle/triangle areas.
6. Explain willingness to pay and the price–quantity relationship; distinguish a movement along demand from a change in demand.
7. Add quantities at the same price to obtain collective demand, with zero rather than negative contributions when appropriate.
8. Interpret supply, its principal determinants and the difference between a price response and a supply shift.
9. Calculate and interpret equilibrium, excess demand or supply, and a new equilibrium after one specified shift.
10. Combine a calculation or graph with a short, source-supported economic explanation, and say when the information does not determine an outcome.

These are proposed curriculum outcomes, not a claim that all students attain them. The authoring plan must distinguish **taught**, **practised independently**, and **demonstrated by this learner**.

## 5. Chapter 1.1 — Economisch denken en rekenen

**Organising question:** How can we explain an economic choice and check a numerical claim?

**Purpose:** Give students useful tools through recognisable situations, not an isolated mathematics course. Introduce the habit of identifying the question, source, unit and comparison before calculating.

**Entry position:** Basic arithmetic may have been encountered earlier, but proficiency should be checked briefly. Economic scarcity, opportunity cost and the interpretation of market representations are new course learning.

### 1.1.1 Schaarste, keuzes en alternatieve kosten

**Role:** Conceptual introduction with modest arithmetic.

**Intended learning:** Identify a scarce resource, distinguish scarcity from a temporary shortage, describe feasible alternatives, and explain the value of the best alternative forgone. Compare options using a stated objective rather than declaring the largest monetary outcome universally best.

**Teaching route:** Begin with a choice involving time, space or a limited budget. Show why not all attractive uses fit. Move from the specific choice to the definition. Use a small allocation table to connect an amount per hour, place or unit to a total. Keep the arithmetic transparent; the intellectual work is identifying the relevant alternative.

**Proposed target brief:** A school activity has one limited resource and two mutually exclusive feasible uses. The source supplies comparable benefits or net amounts per unit. Students calculate the relevant totals, identify the best forgone option and assess a decision against the stated aim. A short explanation distinguishes the alternative's value from merely the cash paid. A familiar farmer allocation can remain as practice if all required amounts are supplied.

**Visual plan:** A resource-to-options diagram and a compact allocation table. Give a fully labelled example, then remove the labels identifying the chosen and forgone alternatives. The target supplies facts but not the reasoning route.

**Retrieval and transfer:** Retrieve multiplication, subtraction and unit reading. This is the source of later opportunity-cost reasoning in trade; formal comparative advantage is not taught here.

**Boundary:** No production-possibility-frontier optimisation, calculated trade ratios or formal profit theory. If a supplied net-benefit or profit relation is used, students are applying provided information, not demonstrating independent Book 2 formula knowledge. Avoid assigning monetary values to enjoyment without making clear that the values are part of the constructed example.

### 1.1.2 Verhoudingen, percentages en indexcijfers

**Role:** Foundational numerical interpretation.

**Intended learning:** Calculate part/whole and per-unit ratios, absolute and relative changes, a new value after a stated percentage change, and an index relative to a named base. Calculate a percentage change between two index values. Distinguish percentage points from percentages and index points from percentages.

**Teaching route:** Begin with competing claims about the same small price or attendance table. Establish “compared with what?” before introducing the formula. Use `(nieuw − oud) / oud × 100%` consistently, retaining a negative sign for a fall. Move to `waarde / basiswaarde × 100` for indexes. Use one simple share example to explain percentage points; do not turn it into a separate statistics course.

**Proposed target brief:** A short source on prices and participation in an activity asks for a per-unit or share calculation, a percentage change, an index, and a change between non-100 indexes. Students then repair a claim that confuses a difference in points with a relative percentage change. Distribute any additional reverse-percentage practice outside this compact target rather than making one unwieldy question.

**Visual plan:** A labelled base-and-change bar followed by the same information on an index scale. A two-column contrast shows, for example, a change from 20% to 25% as five percentage points and 25% relative growth. The final target requires the calculation, not memorisation of the illustration.

**Retrieval and transfer:** Repeat units and simple division from §1.1.1. Revisit percentage changes in every later chapter, particularly comparing old/new demand and equilibrium. Book 2 elasticity depends on this fluency but introduces its own ratio and meaning.

**Boundary and load:** Allow two teaching phases: ratios/percentages first, indexes and comparison bases second. This is not a formal inflation, CPI, purchasing-power or nominal/real lesson. Reverse-percentage calculation is supported practice; repeated growth and rebasing indexes are optional unless later target review explicitly includes them. Never omit the old-value denominator to save space.

### 1.1.3 Tabellen, grafieken en formules

**Role:** Representation skills with a deliberately limited formula bridge.

**Intended learning:** Read headings and units, complete an explicitly requested table, plot given data, interpolate on a stated straight segment and check a source claim. Substitute into a supplied simple linear relation and solve for one missing value. Retrieve rectangle and triangle areas from a diagram with clear base and height.

**Teaching route:** Start from a small table that helps answer a real question, then build its graph. Explain that a market P–Q diagram uses price vertically and quantity horizontally, whereas a time series normally uses time horizontally. Follow the actual units, not an indiscriminate “economics always uses these axes” rule. Introduce the supplied formula as another way of recording a relation. Use one simple inverse calculation, not a general symbolic-rearrangement syllabus.

**Proposed target brief:** A constructed price–quantity table, with other conditions fixed, requires independent graph construction, interpolation and evaluation of a percentage claim. A separate compact item within the same target set asks for substitution/reverse calculation using a supplied relation. A small geometry item checks base and height for a rectangle and triangle. All these operations must already appear in explanation and independent practice; adding them to the new edition is an explicit goal/target revision.

**Visual plan:** Show axes and units, then plotted points, then the segment and reading guides. Use a separate small area figure so the first data graph is not overloaded. Fade from a completed graph to a frame and then independent plotting. A scale choice should remain manageable, not become a hidden puzzle.

**Retrieval and transfer:** Use the percentage method from §1.1.2 in claim checking. A small table-difference example distinguishes a total change from change per unit of the horizontal variable. The economic meanings of cost curves, marginal costs and surplus areas are not assumed from this mathematical encounter.

**Boundary and load:** Plan two teaching phases when necessary. Do not place complete demand-function derivation and two-sided equilibrium solving here as well. No slope-as-elasticity claims. Interpolation from observations is an estimate; connecting many points does not establish causation or an exact universal linear law. In an area task, use the difference between vertical levels for height, not automatically the full axis reading.

### 1.1.4 Gemengde opgaven: economisch denken en rekenen

**Role:** Consolidation, with no new theory.

**Intended learning:** Select the appropriate source and combine choice reasoning, percentages/indexes, graph/table operations and a justified conclusion. Preserve an actual unaided graph-production operation rather than replacing it with graph reading alone.

**Proposed target brief:** A school event provides a limited-resource choice, a small price–quantity table and a promotional claim. Students identify the best alternative forgone under the explicit resource constraint, construct a graph, interpolate, calculate a percentage or index comparison and assess the claim. Keep the choice source and price table visibly separate where they describe different decisions. A short second item retrieves the supplied-formula/area skills without adding a costs chapter.

**Visual and source plan:** No completed target graph. The source has explicit column headers and units; learners create the graph in their exercise book. Use the worked consolidation case to demonstrate how to choose relevant information, then reduce those prompts in the target.

**Boundary and evidence:** No new demand law, equilibrium, elasticity or profit-formula recall. The target is an integration task and needs more time than one short single-operation exercise. Its graph-production and claim-evaluation evidence addresses the substantive mismatch recorded in issue #221. [S7]

## 6. Chapter 1.2 — Vraag

**Organising question:** Why do people buy different amounts, and how do individual decisions become demand on a market?

**Purpose:** Establish demand as a relationship under stated conditions. Give students repeated opportunities to connect an economic explanation with a table, function and graph.

**Entry prerequisites:** The arithmetic, simple formula use, graph reading and plotting of Chapter 1.1. Retrieve these selectively. Willingness to pay and the determinants of demand are new learning.

### 1.2.1 Betalingsbereidheid en individuele vraag

**Role:** Concrete buying decisions followed by a usable linear demand model.

**Intended learning:** Explain willingness to pay for an additional unit and decide which units a buyer purchases at a stated price. Interpret the individual demand relation, calculate quantity at a price and price at a quantity, and identify the intercepts of a simple supplied linear demand function.

**Teaching route:** Begin with a small willingness-to-pay table. A stepped illustration shows separate purchase decisions. Explain that a continuous line is a modelling choice suitable for divisible quantities or an approximation; aggregation does not automatically produce an exactly straight line. Then introduce a simple linear demand model with a clear unit and period, while other circumstances remain unchanged.

**Proposed target brief:** A short buying-decision item establishes the meaning of willingness to pay. A clearly separated linear-demand case then asks students to calculate two coordinates, determine the intercepts, draw the demand line, and explain the direction of the price response. Include a basic price-from-quantity calculation. The two representations need not pretend to be identical data; state explicitly when the linear model is a different case.

**Visual plan:** Willingness-to-pay bars or a small step graph → simple price comparison → separate continuous demand line → matching function and coordinates. Use no more visual detail than each stage needs. In the target, the function may be supplied while the student draws the line.

**Retrieval and transfer:** Retrieve substitution and one-unknown solving from §1.1.3. Here students learn to use both `Q = a − bP` and `P = c − dQ` in a concrete problem. Their equivalence is explained with numbers; a large abstract algebra exercise is unnecessary. This prepares the entry to Book 2 elasticity and consumer surplus.

**Preview boundary:** Keep one small explanation of `betalingsbereidheid − betaalde prijs` as consumer-surplus familiarity, optionally summing a few purchased units. Do not require a formal continuous CS triangle, PS or welfare argument. Propose moving staircase construction out of the independent exit requirement; because the current registry requires it, this target change must be expressly recorded. [S6, S8]

**Precision:** State what the exercise assumes when price equals willingness to pay. Do not infer supply, equilibrium or actual market transactions from a demand function alone. Avoid calling consumer advantage business “profit”.

### 1.2.2 Vraagfactoren: bewegen of verschuiven?

**Role:** A key distinction, taught through controlled contrasts.

**Intended learning:** Distinguish an own-price movement along a given demand curve from a shift caused by another factor. Explain the roles of income, preferences, the price of substitutes/complements and the number of buyers. Interpret expectations only in a short, explicitly specified current-versus-future context.

**Teaching route:** Compare two cases involving the same product. Change its own price first; keep the curve. Then hold that price fixed and change a named demand factor; shift the curve. Distinguish a taste/health-information change from a change in the price of a substitute. For an income change, supply the relevant product behaviour instead of assuming all goods respond identically.

**Proposed target brief:** A concise source includes an own-price change and a separate change in a related good or consumers' preferences. Students classify the changes, name the economically specific cause, indicate the direction in a graph and explain the result. Include a combined-change item: examine each change separately, then say whether their effects reinforce or oppose one another, and reject a plausible incorrect explanation. Do not ask for an exact net quantity without sufficient numerical information.

**Visual plan:** Paired diagrams with identical scales, one illustrating movement and one a shift. Fade the arrows and explanatory labels, then have the learner show the relevant change. The guided route includes the combined-change misconception exercise before the independent target.

**Retrieval and transfer:** Repeat reading quantity at a given price and interpreting a percentage change. These meanings support Book 2's own-price, income and cross-price responsiveness work. No elasticity formula is needed here.

**Boundary:** “Normal” or “inferior” may be explained briefly or supplied in the source, but independent Ei-based classification belongs to Book 2. Do not require the price elasticity of supply. Do not call an initial demand point “market equilibrium” before supply has been introduced. The forward reference leads to collective demand, not to supply.

### 1.2.3 Van individuele naar collectieve vraag

**Role:** Aggregation with bounded, economically meaningful algebra.

**Intended learning:** Add quantities at the same price, calculate collective demand from a table, add simple linear functions where their conditions apply and explain that a buyer contributes zero demand when the price becomes too high.

**Teaching route:** First add two buyers or groups in a labelled table. Show horizontal addition graphically. Next add their simple linear functions in a price interval where both quantities are non-negative. State that interval next to the resulting formula. Finally use one buyer-exit contrast to explain why an unchanged formula cannot be extrapolated indefinitely.

**Proposed target brief:** Two small, internally consistent demand sources require table completion, collective-demand calculation at specified prices and addition of the supplied functions where both buyers participate. Students represent the combined demand and explain, in ordinary language, what changes when one buyer no longer buys. The target need not require a complete piecewise formula or algebraic proof of all intervals.

**Visual plan:** Two individual/group panels plus the total on a matched price scale, or one spacious graph when labels remain clear. Horizontal reading guides highlight “same price, add quantities”. A second price row shows zero from the buyer who exits. Retain the table in a table-reading task; remove worked totals and hints rather than the source itself.

**Retrieval and transfer:** Retrieve linear substitution, collection of like terms and intercepts from §1.2.1. Repeat the meaning of demand shifts when the number of buyers changes. This secures Book 2's use of a collective demand function without prematurely teaching multivariable elasticity models.

**Boundary:** Keep buyer-exit reasoning; remove formal kink terminology, piecewise notation and extensive domain/slope analysis from compulsory outcomes. A straight line remains a stated simplification. Never subtract an economically negative individual demand from another buyer's positive demand. Correct the registry's table/function discrepancy before using that old case. [S7, S8]

### 1.2.4 Gemengde opgaven: vraag

**Role:** Consolidation, with no new theory.

**Intended learning:** Select between individual and collective information, calculate with demand relations, explain a movement or shift, and support a claim with a source or graph.

**Proposed target brief:** A leisure-product case supplies two buyer groups and a short change in circumstances. Students calculate collective demand at a price, use the relevant function, interpret a price or non-price change and write a short evidence-based conclusion. An additional compact source retrieves willingness to pay or the zero-contribution case. Do not make both cases long chains of the same arithmetic.

**Visual and source plan:** Use the diagram or table genuinely required by the question. Include at least one independent graphical action already practised, but do not demand all individual curves, a collective curve and several shifts on one small set of axes.

**Boundary and evidence:** No supply/equilibrium question disguised as demand consolidation; no compulsory surplus or elasticity formula. The independent evidence must cover function use as well as terminology. A learner who can name a substitute but cannot calculate quantity at a given price is not yet ready for the later demand calculations.

## 7. Chapter 1.3 — Aanbod en marktevenwicht

**Organising question:** How do the plans of buyers and sellers determine a price, and what happens when those plans change?

**Purpose:** Complete the market model while keeping prices, desired quantities and actual transactions distinct. Make elementary equation solving and causal reasoning visible before the interventions of Book 3.

**Entry prerequisites:** Individual/collective demand, graph construction, substitution, simple equations and movement-versus-shift reasoning. The meaning of supply and market equilibrium is new.

### 1.3.1 Aanbod en aanbodfactoren

**Role:** Transfer the representation skills to sellers, with a new economic interpretation.

**Intended learning:** Explain the amount sellers are willing and able to offer at different prices. Read and calculate from a supply relation. Distinguish an own-price movement from shifts due to input costs, technology and the number of suppliers. Distinguish supply from inventory and from actual sales.

**Teaching route:** Introduce the seller's decision in a familiar product market. Briefly identify resources used in production, without a separate classification syllabus for all production factors. Show a supply table and line while other circumstances remain unchanged. Introduce a simple function only after the quantities have meaning. Explain why higher input costs can reduce supply at the same selling price; the market selling price is not the input price.

**Proposed target brief:** A producer or market source asks for quantities at two prices and interpretation of a supply function. A cost or technology change then requires a shift explanation and a labelled graphical action. Include a distinction item in which the selling price and an input cost both change: analyse them separately before discussing their combined effect.

**Visual plan:** Demand/supply actor comparison, a seller table and a staged supply graph. Use consistent price–quantity axes. Distinguish one producer's quantity from the whole market quantity in the caption and source, without introducing the competitive firm's horizontal demand curve.

**Retrieval and transfer:** Reuse function/graph operations and the movement/shift distinction. Generic per-unit amounts prepare Book 2 cost interpretation, but supply-as-marginal-cost is not yet independently required.

**Boundary:** State the interval in which a linear supply formula is meaningful; below a stated entry price the offered quantity is zero, not negative. Use simple positive price-intercept examples first. Do not claim all supply curves must have one universal intercept or never touch the quantity axis. No fixed/variable-cost classification, MK optimisation or price-floor analysis here.

### 1.3.2 Marktevenwicht: prijs en hoeveelheid

**Role:** First formal two-sided market equation and adjustment mechanism.

**Intended learning:** Explain equilibrium as agreement between desired demand and supply at one price. Solve `Qv = Qa`, calculate the matching quantity, check both relations, and identify excess demand or excess supply at another stated price.

**Teaching route:** Start with a common-price table: compare buyers' and sellers' plans at each price. Introduce the crossing graph, then write the equality. Model the algebra as equality-preserving operations, not unexplained movement of terms across the equals sign. Substitute the answer into both relations. Return to the source to explain what the numbers mean.

**Proposed target brief:** A simple product market supplies one demand and one supply function. Students calculate price and quantity, verify the result, mark or construct the required market diagram, and calculate the gap at a non-equilibrium price. They explain the direction of pressure on the price under the stated adjustment assumptions. Include a basic actual-sales question only with a clear transaction rule.

**Visual plan:** Table → two lines → intersection → dashed guides. Use a separate shortage/oversupply diagram rather than crowding the first one. Fading moves from completed example, to partial representation, to independent equilibrium work. A supplied answer graph is never labelled as an empty frame.

**Retrieval and transfer:** Reuse linear substitution and one-unknown equations. A two-sided equation is explicitly taught here, not presumed from §1.1.3. It later supports Book 2 break-even algebra by transfer, but Book 2 must explain why its equality is different.

**Boundary:** Equilibrium is not automatically fairness or a proof about total welfare. Do not teach statutory price controls here. At a non-clearing price, `min(Qv, Qa)` describes actual trades only under appropriate matching/no-other-constraint assumptions stated in the exercise; price and two curves alone do not tell us who obtains the products.

### 1.3.3 Verschuivingen en nieuw evenwicht

**Role:** Link a change in circumstances to a recalculated market outcome.

**Intended learning:** Identify which curve changes and why, distinguish that shift from the price-driven response along the unchanged curve, calculate the new equilibrium when relations are supplied, and compare the outcome with the original situation.

**Teaching route:** Hold one curve fixed. Start from a named demand or supply factor and compare quantities at the old price. Explain the temporary mismatch and the direction of price pressure. Then solve the new equality and check whether the signs of the changes match the explanation. Cover a demand and a supply case across the worked example and practice, not necessarily two long cases in one target.

**Proposed target brief:** A short source changes input costs or buyer demand. Students calculate the original equilibrium and the new one, show the graphical change and explain the result using the correct factor. They compare at least one value proportionally or state a relevant unit. A misconception item rejects the claim that the new price, by itself, caused the original curve to shift.

**Visual plan:** Same axes and scale across the old and new situation. Show an arrow for the shifting curve and a different marker for movement along the unchanged curve. The answer must connect source → curve → adjustment → outcome, not just display two intersections.

**Retrieval and transfer:** Reuse percentage changes, algebraic checks and graphical conventions. Include a bounded two-shift contrast in guided practice: when both demand and supply rise, the direction of quantity is determined in the standard model but the price direction requires relative magnitudes. Do not infer missing numbers from the drawing.

**Boundary:** No taxes, subsidies, formal elasticity, externality model or automatic welfare conclusion. Do not require all possible combinations of two shifts as a memorised matrix. Every scenario begins from an explicit baseline; a second question does not silently retain a previous change.

### 1.3.4 Gemengde opgaven: aanbod en marktevenwicht

**Role:** Consolidation and evidence of readiness for the next book; no new theory.

**Intended learning:** Select relevant sources and methods, integrate equilibrium with a change scenario, and explain the outcome with correct units and model limits.

**Proposed target brief:** One compact market case requires an initial equilibrium, a justified curve shift, a new equilibrium and an evidence-based conclusion. A separate short item checks non-equilibrium quantities or a demand intercept. Retrieve per-unit arithmetic, percentage comparison and simple area geometry through a small selection from already-taught formats, not by adding a hidden fourth theory chapter.

**Visual and source plan:** A readable function/table source and one graphical operation at the independent level. Students should have to choose the relevant source, but not decode artificial clutter or missing definitions. A source–question spread may be used in the eventual print layout.

**Book 2 handoff:** Gather the readiness evidence across all three mixed paragraphs plus the relevant earlier targets. Do not force every skill into this one final exercise. Any persistent gap receives a specific printed practice reference; it is not diagnosed merely from a self-rating checklist.

**Boundary:** This section may use a supplied revenue/cost relation for substitution, but does not require selecting an untaught profit formula or interpreting break-even. It neither teaches elasticity nor promotes the earlier surplus preview into formal welfare knowledge.

## 11. Common authoring and exercise contract

For the new edition, adopt the Book 2+ printed sequence as an explicit design choice. The existing repository contract remains non-retroactive to the first edition. [S6, S9]

**Printed order:** motivating question and lesson goals → explanation → **Uitgewerkt voorbeeld** → compact non-heading summary → **Startopgaven** → **Begeleide inoefening** → **Zelfstandige oefening** → **Doeloefening** → **Denkertje / Bonusopgave** → **Herhaling / Herhaling en interleaving**.

The seven bold exercise headings after the explanation are used consistently. The target is the destination, not an opening examination of material that has not yet been taught. A short statement of the lesson's purpose can still appear at the beginning.

### Backward design and support

For each theory paragraph, create the goal–target-operation–worked-example–start–guided–independent alignment table before drafting the prose. Every target operation is either explicitly taught or retrieved from identified earlier teaching. A preview does not fill an independent-coverage cell.

The core route is **Startopgaven → Zelfstandige oefening → Doeloefening**. A teacher may assign the prerequisite part of Startopgaven at entry. The printed order does not change. Guided practice is optional support toward the same end goal, not a separate easier curriculum.

When students must produce a graph, begin the support route with a correct completed representation, then provide a partial frame, then remove production support. When the target asks them to read a supplied graph or table, retain that source and fade only markings and hints. Never equate “less support” with removing information required to answer the question.

Use varied but recognisable contexts. Do not add new economic meaning without an explicit bridge. A bonus changes a representation, assumption or way of reasoning; it is not merely a longer version of the compulsory calculation. Closing review contains one or two short tasks on previously taught material.

### Answer construction

Calculation answers show the relation or procedure, substitution, result, unit and relevant interpretation. Explanations name the actor and mechanism and cite the specific source information. Do not accept a direction arrow as a complete explanation when the question asks “leg uit”. Keep the procedure consistent between example, practice and answer model.

Provide complete answer graphs separately. Distinguish reading support from accidentally disclosing an independent exercise's answer. Where a table is blank, explicitly ask students to complete it before asking about the completed values.

### Paper and visual quality

Everything essential is on paper: explanation, source, example, support and practice. No QR code, website visit or additional device is needed to complete the core. A calculator and exercise book are ordinary classroom tools, not an online dependency.

Give axes variables, units and readable scales. Distinguish total euro amounts from euro amounts per unit. Keep matched comparison diagrams on the same scale, directly label curves, and verify all numerical points against their functions. Define whether a diagram is schematic or numerically exact. Cover art must not serve as the mathematical reference.
